# AI-Assisted Research and Fact-Checking Workflow

> **Version:** 1.0  
> **Last updated:** July 2026  
> **Scope:** Marketing research, editorial verification, product claims, competitor analysis and source-based content

## Purpose
This workflow supports structured research and fact-checking with the assistance of generative AI.

It is designed to help researchers and editors:
- define a research question,
- identify claims requiring evidence,
- find and assess relevant sources,
- distinguish facts from interpretations,
- detect unsupported or outdated information,
- compare conflicting evidence,
- document verification decisions,
- prepare content that can be reviewed by a human editor.

AI can support source discovery, organization and analysis, but it should not be treated as an independent source of truth.

## Use cases
This workflow can be used for:
- marketing research,
- competitor analysis,
- product and feature verification,
- trend analysis,
- editorial fact-checking,
- social media content verification,
- newsletter research,
- industry reports,
- campaign planning,
- content audits,
- brand communication,
- AI-generated content review,
- checking statistics and numerical claims,
- verifying quotations and public statements,
- confirming dates, names and terminology,
- identifying outdated information.

## Core definitions

### Fact

A statement that can be checked against reliable evidence.

Example:

> The report was published in July 2026.

### Claim

A statement presented as true that requires evidence.

Example:

> The campaign increased customer engagement.

### Interpretation

An explanation of what verified information may mean.

Example:
> The increase may suggest that shorter videos were more effective for this audience.

### Inference

A conclusion drawn from available evidence that is not stated directly in the source.

Inferences must be clearly labelled and should not be presented as confirmed facts.

### Assumption

Information treated as true without sufficient evidence.

Assumptions should be identified and either verified or removed.

### Unknown

Information that cannot currently be verified using the available sources.

An unknown should remain marked as unresolved rather than being completed with a plausible answer.

## Input requirements
Before beginning the research, provide:
- the main research question,
- the intended use of the research,
- the target audience,
- the required publication or delivery date,
- the geographic scope,
- the relevant time period,
- the preferred source types,
- excluded source types,
- confirmed background information,
- claims that require verification,
- required output format,
- legal, confidentiality or compliance restrictions,
- the acceptable level of uncertainty.

## Research question framework
A useful research question should define:
- **Topic:** What is being investigated?
- **Purpose:** Why is the information needed?
- **Audience:** Who will use the findings?
- **Scope:** Which markets, products, companies or channels are included?
- **Time period:** What dates are relevant?
- **Decision:** What action should the research support?

Example:

> Identify the most relevant social media content trends for European construction brands in July 2026 and explain which trends may create practical opportunities for contractor-focused communication.

## Source hierarchy

Not every source should be treated as equally reliable.

### Tier 1 — Primary and official sources

Preferred for important factual claims:

- legislation and regulatory documents,
- government websites,
- official company documentation,
- financial filings,
- original research papers,
- official datasets,
- standards and technical documentation,
- court decisions,
- official product documentation,
- direct statements from authorized representatives,
- original reports published by the organization that conducted the research.

### Tier 2 — Reputable secondary sources

Useful for explanation, context and independent reporting:

- established news organizations,
- peer-reviewed review papers,
- recognized industry associations,
- respected research institutes,
- professional publications with transparent editorial standards,
- specialist media with named authors and disclosed sources.

### Tier 3 — Discovery and context sources

Useful for finding leads, but usually insufficient for confirming important claims:
- personal blogs,
- company blogs discussing third-party topics,
- newsletters,
- content aggregators,
- social media posts,
- online forums,
- user reviews,
- AI-generated summaries,
- unattributed articles,
- search-result snippets.

Tier 3 sources may help identify a topic, keyword or original source, but should not automatically be treated as evidence.

## Source assessment criteria
Each important source should be evaluated for:
1. **Authority**  
   Who created the source, and are they qualified to discuss the topic?
2. **Proximity to the evidence**  
   Is this the original source or a summary of another publication?
3. **Recency**  
   Is the information current enough for the research question?
4. **Transparency**  
   Are the author, publication date, methodology and evidence visible?
5. **Evidence quality**  
   Does the source provide data, references, documents or a clear methodology?
6. **Independence**  
   Is the source independent, or does it have a commercial or political interest?
7. **Specificity**  
   Does the source support the exact claim being made?
8. **Context**  
   Are the market, geography, population and time period relevant?
9. **Consistency**  
   Does the information agree with other reliable sources?
10. **Limitations**  
    Does the source clearly communicate uncertainty or methodological constraints?

## Recommended workflow
**Research brief → Claim identification → Source plan → Source collection → Evidence assessment → Claim verification → Conflict analysis → Synthesis → Human review → Publication**

## 1. Define the research brief

Before searching, document:
- the main question,
- the intended decision,
- the target audience,
- the research scope,
- the deadline,
- the required level of detail,
- the acceptable source types,
- the expected output.

A clear brief reduces irrelevant research and prevents the scope from expanding unnecessarily.

## 2. Break the topic into verifiable claims
Do not research a broad topic as one single question.

Create a list of smaller claims or sub-questions.

Example:
Instead of:
> What are the current AI trends?

Break the topic into:
- Which new AI tools were launched during the selected period?
- Which product updates were announced by official sources?
- Which use cases are relevant to marketing teams?
- Which claims are supported by adoption data?
- Which trends are based mainly on commentary or predictions?

## 3. Prepare a source plan

For each claim, identify the preferred source type.

| Claim type | Preferred source |
|---|---|
| Product feature | Official product documentation |
| Law or regulation | Official legal text or regulator |
| Company performance | Financial filing or official report |
| Scientific finding | Original research paper |
| Market statistic | Original dataset or research methodology |
| Public statement | Original interview, speech or press release |
| Current event | Reputable reporting and primary statements |
| Industry trend | Multiple independent sources and current examples |

## 4. Collect sources
During source collection:
- prioritize original documents,
- record the title and publisher,
- record the publication date,
- record the date the source was accessed,
- save the relevant section or page number,
- identify the author when available,
- note whether the source is primary or secondary,
- record any visible conflict of interest.

Do not rely only on:
- search-result snippets,
- AI-generated summaries,
- headlines,
- social media screenshots,
- unattributed statistics,
- references copied from another article without opening the original source.

## 5. Read the source in context
Before using a source, check:
- what the full document says,
- whether the headline accurately reflects the content,
- whether the relevant statement is presented as fact, opinion or prediction,
- whether the source includes important limitations,
- whether the information applies to the correct market and time period,
- whether the quoted number refers to the correct sample or population.

A source may be credible but still irrelevant to the specific claim.

## 6. Build a claim-evidence register

Every important claim should be recorded with its supporting evidence.

### Claim-evidence register template

| ID | Claim | Source | Source type | Evidence status | Evidence strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | [Claim] | [Source title] | Primary / Secondary | Supported / Partial / Contradicted / Unverified | High / Medium / Low | [Context or limitation] |

## Evidence statuses

### Supported

The available source directly supports the claim.

### Partially supported

The source supports only part of the claim, or the wording is stronger than the evidence.

### Contradicted

A reliable source provides evidence that conflicts with the claim.

### Unverified

No sufficient evidence has been found.

### Outdated

The claim may have been correct previously, but the available evidence is no longer current.

### Requires specialist review

The claim involves legal, medical, financial, technical or regulatory interpretation beyond the scope of editorial fact-checking.

## Evidence strength

### High

- supported by a relevant primary source,
- current,
- directly connected to the claim,
- methodology or evidence is transparent.

### Medium

- supported by a reputable secondary source,
- some context or methodological information is missing,
- the claim is reasonable but not fully documented.

### Low

- supported only by indirect, promotional or unattributed material,
- source quality is uncertain,
- information cannot be independently confirmed.

## 7. Verify quotations
For every quotation:
- locate the original source,
- confirm the exact wording,
- check the speaker or author,
- confirm the date,
- review the surrounding context,
- avoid changing the meaning through shortening,
- clearly mark paraphrases as paraphrases.

Do not use quotation marks around text reconstructed or summarized by an AI model.

## 8. Verify numerical claims
For every statistic or numerical claim, check:
- the original dataset or report,
- the measurement period,
- the sample size,
- the geographic scope,
- the definition of the metric,
- whether the number is an estimate,
- whether percentages use the correct base,
- whether the comparison uses equivalent periods,
- whether the result is statistically or practically meaningful.

A number should not be used only because it appears in several articles. Multiple articles may repeat the same unsupported source.

## 9. Check dates and freshness
For time-sensitive information:
- record the publication date,
- distinguish publication date from the date of the event or data,
- check whether a newer source is available,
- confirm whether the webpage has been updated,
- avoid describing older information as current,
- include the research cut-off date in the final output.

Example:
> Information verified up to 29 July 2026.

## 10. Handle conflicting evidence
When reliable sources disagree:
1. Do not hide the disagreement.
2. Compare publication dates.
3. Compare definitions and methodologies.
4. Check whether the sources cover different markets or populations.
5. Look for the original data.
6. Identify commercial or institutional interests.
7. Prefer the source closest to the original evidence.
8. Explain which interpretation is better supported.
9. Mark the issue as unresolved when the evidence remains inconclusive.

The final content should not create false certainty.

## 11. Distinguish evidence from interpretation
The final research should clearly separate:
- verified facts,
- source statements,
- interpretations,
- inferences,
- recommendations,
- unresolved questions.

Recommended labels:
- **Verified fact**
- **Source-reported claim**
- **Interpretation**
- **Inference**
- **Requires verification**
- **Unknown**

## 12. Prepare the synthesis
A useful research summary should explain:
- what was found,
- which findings are strongly supported,
- which claims remain uncertain,
- where sources disagree,
- why the findings matter,
- what decision or action they may support,
- what additional verification is required.

The synthesis should not simply repeat source summaries.

## 13. Conduct final human review
Before publication, verify:
- every important claim against its source,
- every quotation against the original text,
- every statistic against the correct methodology,
- the date and relevance of each source,
- whether citations support the exact wording,
- whether interpretations are labelled,
- whether uncertainty is communicated honestly,
- whether confidential information has been removed.

## Reusable prompt template
```text
You are a senior research editor and fact-checker responsible for preparing source-based content.

Your task is to research the topic below, assess the quality of the available evidence and prepare a transparent, verifiable summary.

RESEARCH BRIEF

- Main research question: [write the question]
- Intended use: [article / report / campaign / presentation / social media / internal brief]
- Target audience: [describe the audience]
- Geographic scope: [market or region]
- Time period: [relevant dates]
- Research cut-off date: [date]
- Required level of detail: [short / standard / detailed]
- Preferred source types: [official documentation / original research / reputable media / other]
- Excluded source types: [list or write "none"]
- Required output format: [describe the format]
- Legal, confidentiality or compliance requirements: [add requirements or write "not provided"]

CONFIRMED BACKGROUND INFORMATION

[List information already verified, or write "not provided"]

CLAIMS TO VERIFY

[List the claims, or ask the model to extract claims from the supplied content]

CONTENT OR TOPIC TO RESEARCH

[Paste the content or describe the topic]

SOURCE REQUIREMENTS

- Prioritize primary and official sources.
- Use reputable secondary sources for independent context.
- Use blogs, aggregators, social media and forums only as discovery sources unless no stronger evidence exists.
- Open and assess the original source rather than relying on a search-result snippet or third-party summary.
- Record the publisher, author when available, publication date and source type.
- Prefer current sources when the topic is time-sensitive.
- Do not treat an AI-generated answer as evidence.

RESEARCH PROCESS

1. Clarify the scope of the question.
2. Break the topic into individual claims or sub-questions.
3. Identify the preferred source type for each claim.
4. Gather relevant evidence.
5. Evaluate each source for authority, recency, transparency, independence and relevance.
6. Compare the source with the exact wording of the claim.
7. Identify conflicts, gaps and outdated information.
8. Separate facts, interpretations, inferences and unknowns.
9. Prepare a transparent synthesis.
10. List all remaining verification requirements.

OUTPUT FORMAT

### 1. Research scope

Summarize:
- the research question,
- scope,
- time period,
- source strategy,
- important limitations.

### 2. Key findings

Present the most important findings in order of relevance.

For each finding, state whether it is:
- verified,
- partially verified,
- contradicted,
- unverified,
- outdated,
- requiring specialist review.

### 3. Claim-evidence register

Create a table with the following columns:
- Claim ID
- Claim
- Evidence status
- Supporting or contradicting source
- Source type
- Publication date
- Evidence strength
- Notes and limitations

### 4. Source assessment

For every important source, assess:
- authority,
- proximity to the original evidence,
- recency,
- transparency,
- independence,
- relevance to the claim.

### 5. Conflicting information

List any disagreement between reliable sources.

Explain whether the disagreement may result from:
- different dates,
- different definitions,
- different methodology,
- different geography,
- different populations,
- commercial or institutional interests.

Do not choose one version without explaining the evidence.

### 6. Claims requiring further verification

List all claims that cannot be confidently confirmed.

For each claim, explain:
- what evidence is missing,
- which source type would be needed,
- whether the claim should be removed, softened or marked as uncertain.

### 7. Source-based summary

Prepare a concise summary using only information supported by the reviewed evidence.

Clearly label:
- interpretations,
- inferences,
- recommendations,
- unresolved questions.

### 8. Human review checklist

Mark each item as:
- Complete
- Requires review
- Not applicable

Checklist:
- source opened and reviewed,
- primary source identified,
- publication date checked,
- claim supported by cited evidence,
- quotations verified,
- numerical data verified,
- geographic scope confirmed,
- methodology reviewed,
- conflicting evidence disclosed,
- interpretation labelled,
- outdated information removed,
- confidential information removed,
- final editorial approval completed.

IMPORTANT RULES

- Do not invent sources, authors, quotations, statistics or publication details.
- Do not cite a source that has not been opened and assessed.
- Do not treat a search-result snippet as sufficient evidence.
- Do not use an AI-generated statement as a source.
- Do not assume that repeated information is independently verified.
- Do not confirm a claim unless the cited source supports the exact wording.
- Do not hide conflicting evidence.
- Clearly distinguish facts from interpretations and inferences.
- Mark unresolved information as unknown or requiring verification.
- Do not expose confidential, personal or restricted information.
- Treat the final output as a research draft requiring human approval.
```

## Expected output

The workflow should produce:
- a clearly defined research scope,
- a list of verifiable claims,
- a documented source strategy,
- a claim-evidence register,
- an assessment of source quality,
- a list of contradictions and uncertainty,
- a source-based summary,
- a human verification checklist.

## Human review checklist

Before using the research in published content, confirm that:
- every key claim has been checked against the original source,
- quotations are accurate and shown in context,
- numerical data uses the correct period and definition,
- sources are current enough for the research question,
- primary sources were used where available,
- secondary sources were used appropriately,
- conflicts between sources were disclosed,
- assumptions and inferences were labelled,
- unsupported claims were removed or softened,
- confidential information was excluded,
- citations support the exact wording,
- the final content has received editorial approval.

## Data privacy and confidentiality

Before submitting research materials to an AI tool:
- remove unnecessary personal information,
- anonymize confidential names and identifiers,
- exclude unpublished company data,
- do not upload restricted documents to unapproved systems,
- follow internal data-processing and AI policies,
- confirm whether the selected tool is approved for the source material,
- separate public research from confidential internal analysis.

The researcher remains responsible for deciding whether information can be safely processed by an external AI system.

## Limitations

This workflow does not replace:
- professional legal review,
- medical or scientific expert review,
- financial or investment analysis,
- formal academic peer review,
- statistical validation,
- cybersecurity assessment,
- access to paid or restricted databases,
- interviews with subject-matter experts,
- human editorial judgment.

AI may:
- overlook relevant sources,
- misinterpret evidence,
- create inaccurate citations,
- confuse publication dates,
- present uncertain information confidently,
- summarize a source inaccurately,
- fail to recognize outdated information.

Webpages, product documentation and public statements may also change after the research is completed.
